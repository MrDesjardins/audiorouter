01 / Understand

The idea, before the settings.

Ten fixed-center faders control 31.5, 63, 125, 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000 and 16000 Hz. Their overlapping boosts and cuts combine into one response curve.

Broadband source→Graphic EQ→Meter → Output

Use it in AudioRouter

  1. Start with all ten bands at 0 dB and identify a specific problem by listening.
  2. Read the band descriptions below; change one relevant fader by 1–3 dB.
  3. Compare Input reference with Output, then check neighboring bands only if the correction needs a broader shape.
  4. After boosts, check downstream peaks and reduce upstream Gain if necessary.

What success looks like

Each fixed band shapes an overlapping region: low faders affect weight, middle faders body and articulation, high faders brightness.

If it does not work

A 16 kHz boost may be barely audible on your hardware. Do not compensate by adding extreme gain; use material containing that range.

Select the node to open Properties. Click its name to rename it. While playing, supported parameter-only edits save after a short pause. For structural edits, use Save when the canvas marks a change. Enabled and Bypass are distinct live controls; see the in-app help for their behavior.

03 / Configure

Every control, with a reason.

01

31.5 Hz

band0Db

31.5 Hz controls the very bottom of the sub-bass range. Most voice and everyday playback has little useful content here; this band mainly changes deep rumble and extreme low-end weight.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeCut a little to reduce floor, handling or mechanical rumble.

↑ Higher / alternativeBoost only if your source has clean sub-bass and your speakers can reproduce it.

Try thisTry −3 dB while listening for rumble between phrases; compare at matched loudness.

Keep in mind Large boosts here consume headroom and may move a speaker or headphone driver without making the sound clearer.

02

63 Hz

band1Db

63 Hz adds or reduces deep bass weight: kick-drum thump, bass fundamentals and low cinematic impact often live around here.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeReduce it when kick or bass feels boomy or overwhelms the voice.

↑ Higher / alternativeAdd a small amount when a clean source sounds too thin at the bottom.

Try thisTry +2 dB on a bass-heavy music loop, then check that speech remains easy to hear.

Keep in mind A boost can make small speakers distort; this band is not a fix for room rumble above it.

03

125 Hz

band2Db

125 Hz shapes bass body and warmth. It can add fullness to a voice or instrument, and too much can make a mix sound thick or boomy.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeTrim a few dB if voices sound tubby or bass notes blur together.

↑ Higher / alternativeAdd a little if a close-miked voice or instrument feels too lean.

Try thisTry −2 dB on a boomy voice recording and compare without changing playback volume.

Keep in mind Listen in context: a cut that cleans one voice may thin a full mix.

04

250 Hz

band3Db

250 Hz affects low-mid warmth and the boxy, muddy quality that can build up when several sounds share the same space.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeCut slightly when a voice sounds cloudy or a mix has a cardboard-like buildup.

↑ Higher / alternativeBoost gently when a source lacks body and sounds small.

Try thisTry −2 dB on a muddy voice-plus-music mix, then bypass to confirm clarity improved.

Keep in mind Broad cuts can make a source hollow; change one band at a time.

05

500 Hz

band4Db

500 Hz shapes lower-mid body and woody or boxy tone. It often changes whether a voice or instrument sounds solid or congested.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeCut a little if a source sounds nasal, hollow-boxed or congested in the lower mids.

↑ Higher / alternativeBoost modestly if it has lost body after other cuts.

Try thisTry a small −2 dB cut on a boxy voice, then restore it if the voice becomes thin.

Keep in mind The label is a center frequency; the fixed band also affects nearby frequencies.

06

1 kHz

band5Db

1 kHz sits in the middle of the sound and changes a source’s core, forward tone. Too much can sound honky; too little can push a voice back.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeReduce it slightly if a voice or instrument has a persistent honk.

↑ Higher / alternativeAdd a little if the midrange feels distant after level matching.

Try thisTry −1.5 dB on a honky voice, then compare at the same loudness.

Keep in mind Do not use this band alone to solve intelligibility; consonant clarity is higher in frequency.

07

2 kHz

band6Db

2 kHz affects upper-mid articulation and attack. It can help words and instrument edges come forward, but can also make a sound hard.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeCut slightly if speech or instruments feel aggressive or tiring.

↑ Higher / alternativeBoost gently when a voice is clear but its articulation is buried.

Try thisTry +1.5 dB on a voice that needs a little more definition, then listen for harshness.

Keep in mind A large boost can exaggerate room noise and make listening fatiguing.

08

4 kHz

band7Db

4 kHz adds or reduces presence and crisp definition. This is where a small adjustment can make a voice feel closer, or make it sound sharp.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeCut a little if a voice has a hard edge or the mix is tiring.

↑ Higher / alternativeAdd a little when speech lacks presence against background sound.

Try thisTry +1 dB and compare at matched loudness; keep it only if words are easier to follow.

Keep in mind Strong boosts can emphasize consonant edges and fatigue; sibilance often needs a more targeted tool.

09

8 kHz

band8Db

8 kHz changes brightness and the sense of air, along with some sibilance and hiss. It can make cymbals or consonants more apparent.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeReduce it if “s” sounds, cymbals or recording hiss are too prominent.

↑ Higher / alternativeBoost subtly if a clean source sounds dull and needs a little sparkle.

Try thisTry −2 dB on a hissy voice recording and check that consonants remain natural.

Keep in mind This band can make noise more obvious; de-essing or noise reduction may be more suitable for a specific problem.

10

16 kHz

band9Db

16 kHz changes the extreme top end: a little “air” on sources that contain it. Many speakers, headphones and recordings have little useful energy this high.

Range / choices-18 dB → 18 dBDefault0 dB

↓ Lower / alternativeCut if the top end carries hiss or fizz.

↑ Higher / alternativeA tiny boost can add air to a clean, full-band source.

Try thisCompare a subtle +1 dB change on a bright, clean recording; if you hear no benefit, leave it flat.

Keep in mind Large boosts add noise and consume headroom, and may not be audible on your playback system.

04 / Put it to work

Start with a useful example.

Example 1

Make a small tonal correction

  1. Start with every fader at 0 dB.
  2. Try cutting 250 Hz by 2 dB for excess low-mid body.
  3. Move one fader at a time and compare bypass.
Example 2

Reduce excessive brightness

  1. Try −2 dB at 8 kHz.
  2. Listen to consonants as well as hiss.
  3. Prefer a small cut before combining several deep cuts.

These are starting points. Compare at a comfortable monitoring level and adjust for your own source.

05 / Get unstuck

Small details that matter.

  • A fixed band is not a surgical notch.
  • Multiple boosts add up and can overload later stages.
